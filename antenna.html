An antenna is a length, and the length is a wavelength

Almost every antenna question turns out to be the same question wearing a different hat: how long is this thing compared to the wave it's trying to launch? Get comfortable with that one ratio and impedance, SWR, gain, pattern and take-off angle all stop being folklore. Pick a band up top — every calculator, plot and dimension on this page follows it.

Step 00 · the one numberWavelength

λ = 300 / f

A radio wave travels 300 million metres per second. If it wiggles 146 million times per second, each wiggle is about two metres long. That's the entire derivation: λ metres = 300 ÷ f MHz. Memorise that and you can size an antenna in your head on a hilltop with no signal.

Antennas are built from simple fractions of it — a half wave, a quarter wave, five-eighths of a wave — because those are the lengths where the current on the wire arranges itself into a standing pattern that radiates well. A wire that isn't near one of those fractions still radiates, but it presents an awkward impedance to your radio and you have to work to feed it.

Why 468

You'll see 468 / f quoted for a half-wave dipole in feet. That's just 300/f converted to feet, halved, and multiplied by the 0.95 end-effect fudge. It's a starting point for cutting, not a law — nearby metal, height and wire diameter all move it.

Cut long, trim short

You can always remove wire. Cut a few percent long, measure where it resonates, then trim. Resonance below your target means the antenna is too long, which is the more forgiving mistake to make.

The pocket check

At 146 MHz a quarter wave is about 19 inches, and at 446 MHz about 6.5. If someone hands you a "dual band" whip that's six inches long, you now know which band it's actually resonant on.

Step 01 · what the current is doingStanding waves on the wire

drag the length

Feed a wire in the middle and current flows out towards both ends. It can't flow off the ends, so it reflects and comes back, and the outgoing and returning waves add into a fixed pattern — a standing wave. Where that pattern puts a current maximum and where it puts a current node decides almost everything about how the antenna behaves.

Current makes the radiation

Radiation comes from accelerating charge, which means the parts of the antenna carrying the most current do most of the radiating. On a half-wave dipole that's the middle. The last few inches near the tips carry almost no current and contribute almost nothing — which is why the exact tip shape barely matters, and why loading coils near the base of a short mobile whip are a compromise.

Why the feedpoint impedance swings

Impedance at the feedpoint is just voltage divided by current there. Land the feedpoint on a current maximum and you get a low, friendly impedance near 73 ohms. Land it on a current node — a full wavelength of wire, say — and current goes to nearly zero while voltage peaks, and the impedance rockets into the kilohms. Same wire, same feed, wildly different load.

Step 02 · the matchImpedance, reflection and SWR

50 Ω is a convention

Your radio and your coax expect 50 ohms. Your antenna presents whatever it presents. When those differ, some of the power arriving at the antenna turns around and travels back down the line. Forward and reflected waves interfere along the cable, producing peaks and troughs of voltage — and the ratio of the biggest peak to the smallest trough is the standing wave ratio.

What SWR does and doesn't cost you

The reflected power is not burned up at the antenna. It travels back to the transmitter and most of it gets re-reflected forward again. On a short, low-loss run at VHF, a 2:1 SWR costs you a fraction of a decibel of real power — well under a quarter of an S-unit, invisible on the other end.

What high SWR actually does is: make your transmitter's protection circuit throttle output, raise voltage and current peaks in a lossy cable so a bigger share is turned into heat, and tell you something has changed. That last one is the real value. A jump in SWR is a broken connector, a wet coax end or a bent element long before it is a power problem.

1.0:1 is not the goal

A resonant half-wave dipole in free space is about 73 ohms, which on 50-ohm coax reads 1.46:1 forever. That antenna is working perfectly. A quarter-wave ground plane with horizontal radials is nearer 36 ohms, about 1.4:1 — and drooping those radials down to 45° raises the feedpoint impedance to roughly 50, which is why almost every commercial ground plane has sloping radials.

Be suspicious of a flat 1.0:1 across an entire band. Perfect matches over wide bandwidth usually mean something is absorbing power rather than radiating it — a lossy cable, a poor connection, or a dummy load you forgot to unplug.

Trim trainer · SWR across the band

Slide the element length and watch the dip move. This is the whole trimming procedure: find where the dip actually sits, compare it to where you want it, and cut. Notice too what element thickness does — a fat tube has a lower Q and a much broader curve, which is why a chunky commercial whip covers a whole band and a thin wire dipole is fussy.

Loss budget

The illusion that fools everyone

Your SWR meter lives at the radio, but the mismatch lives at the antenna. The reflected wave has to travel the whole cable twice, losing a little each way — so a lossy line makes a bad antenna look good. Three decibels of cable loss turns a genuine 5:1 antenna into a comfortable-looking 2:1 on the meter in your shack.

Buying advice, compressed

Loss rises with frequency and falls with cable diameter. On HF almost anything works. On 2 metres, RG-8X is fine for short runs and LMR-400 is the sensible default for a roof. On 70 centimetres, thin cable is simply not an option for any real distance — and a run of RG-174 pigtail inside a mag mount can quietly cost you more than the antenna gains.

Connectors matter too. A corroded PL-259 or a joint that got rained on will show up as creeping loss and drifting SWR, and it will always fail on the day of the event.

Step 04 · where the energy goesPatterns, gain and take-off angle

gain is redistribution

An antenna cannot create power. All "gain" means is that energy which would have gone somewhere useless has been pushed somewhere useful. A dipole beats an imaginary isotropic radiator by 2.15 dB only because it refuses to radiate off its ends. A beam gets 7 dB by refusing to radiate almost anywhere except forward — and then you have to point it.

Height is measured in wavelengths

Thirty feet is nothing on 40 metres and nearly five wavelengths on 2 metres. A horizontal antenna's pattern is set by interference with its own reflection in the ground, so the same mast produces completely different lobes depending on the band. Change bands up top and watch the plot rearrange itself without touching the height slider.

dBi, dBd and marketing

dBi compares to a theoretical point source; dBd compares to a dipole. They differ by 2.15 dB, and vendors quote whichever flatters. If a handheld whip claims "3 dB gain" with no reference and no pattern, assume the number is optimistic and the antenna is a compromise — which is fine, because a handheld is a compromise.

Verticals don't lobe the same way

A vertical with its own radial system carries its ground plane with it, so its shape is largely set by the antenna rather than the height. Raising it still helps enormously — but through horizon and obstruction clearance, not by reshaping the lobes.

Step 05 · orientationPolarisation

free decibels, or free losses

A vertical antenna launches a vertically polarised wave, and a receiving antenna at right angles to it responds very poorly. This costs nothing to get right and a great deal to get wrong, and it's the reason for a convention every new Technician runs into: FM and repeaters are vertical, weak-signal SSB and CW work on VHF is horizontal.

In the real world the loss levels off around 20 to 25 dB rather than going to infinity, because reflections off buildings and terrain scramble polarisation on the way. That's why holding a handheld sideways still works across town — and why it stops working the moment you need the last few dB on a distant repeater.

Hold a handheld upright, not tilted. Tilting it 45° throws away 3 dB before the signal has left the room, and your body absorbs more.

Honest comparison

If you own a handheld and nothing else, the highest-value purchase is not a more powerful radio. It's almost anything that gets a real antenna away from your body and higher up — a roll-up J-pole in a tree with a length of decent coax will routinely turn an unusable repeater into a full-quieting one, for the price of a pizza.

Things that will bite you

field notes
  • Never transmit without a loadKeying up with no antenna, or a disconnected coax, reflects everything straight back into the finals. Modern radios protect themselves, older and cheaper ones don't always. Check the connector before you check anything else.
  • Water is the enemyCoax wicks. An unsealed connector at the top of a mast will draw water down the cable over months and quietly ruin the whole run. Seal outdoor joints properly and put a drip loop below the connector.
  • Your coax braid is part of the antennaCurrent can flow back down the outside of the shield, making the feedline radiate. The pattern skews, the SWR changes when you touch the cable, and RF turns up in the shack. A choke balun — even a few turns of coax through a ferrite — usually fixes it.
  • Your hand is an antenna componentOn a handheld, your body is the counterpoise. Grip changes the tuning and absorbs power. This is why the same radio performs differently held at your face and held out at arm's length, and why a tigertail or a real antenna on a cable helps so much.
  • Measure at the antenna when you canAn analyser at the feedpoint tells the truth. The same analyser at the end of a long run tells you a flattering story. If the two disagree, the cable is the difference — and that difference is loss.
  • RF exposure is your responsibilityEvery amateur station has to be evaluated for RF exposure — the old blanket exemption for hams went away in 2021. For a Technician running low power on VHF it is usually a short calculation and a note in a folder, but it does have to be done, and antenna placement relative to people is the main variable.
  • Antennas are seasonalWet trees detune wire antennas, ice adds weight and changes resonance, and a summer-tuned dipole in a leafy yard reads differently in February. If your SWR shifts a few percent with the weather, nothing is broken.
  • A dummy load has a perfect matchIt's also a resistor, and it radiates essentially nothing. Any time an SWR reading looks suspiciously ideal, ask what's absorbing the power.

What to buy first

An analyser or a cheap NanoVNA changes everything, because it turns guessing into measuring. Second is good coax. Third is height. A more expensive radio comes a long way after all three.
