From chirp to plaintext

A message crosses several worlds on its way to you: a frequency sweep in the ISM band, a LoRa frame, 255 bytes of MeshCore packet, a route made of truncated public keys, and finally an AES block that only your device can open. This page walks up that stack one layer at a time, and ends with you decoding raw hex by hand.

Layer 00 · physicalWhat a packet sounds like

chirp spread spectrum

LoRa doesn't send bits as voltage levels. It sends chirps — tones that sweep linearly across the channel bandwidth. A symbol is one full sweep, and the data is encoded in where in the band that sweep starts. Because a chirp is spread across the whole channel, a receiver can still pull it out from under the noise floor, which is why these radios work at ranges where the signal is weaker than the noise around it.

A spreading factor of SF9 means each symbol carries 9 bits and lasts 2⁹ chirp steps. Every step up in SF doubles both the airtime and the noise immunity. That single trade governs everything above it: how long you hold the channel, how many repeaters can talk, and how much of your 255-byte budget is worth spending.

Time on air

Why it matters upstream

Airtime is the scarce resource, not bandwidth. A flood packet is retransmitted by every repeater that hears it, so one message at SF12 can occupy the channel for seconds across a whole region. This is the real reason MeshCore separates clients from repeaters.

Duty cycle

In much of Europe the 868 MHz sub-bands are capped at 1% or 10% transmit time. Repeater firmware enforces a silent period proportional to what it just sent, so a long packet buys itself a long wait. Firmware 1.15 tightened this to match the legal limit properly.

Listen before talk

Before transmitting, the dispatcher checks whether the radio is already receiving, and schedules around it. Combined with randomised flood delays, this is what keeps a burst of simultaneous rebroadcasts from colliding into mutual noise.

None of this is negotiated per-packet. Every node in a network must be configured with the same frequency, bandwidth, spreading factor and coding rate, or they simply cannot hear each other — there is no discovery at this layer. Many regions have converged on narrow bandwidths, 62.5 kHz with SF7–SF9, which fits between ISM-band interference and gives a lower noise floor than the wider LoRaWAN-style channels.

Offset 0x00The header byte

0bVVPPPPRR

One byte carries three things. Bit 0 is the rightmost bit, so reading the byte left to right gives you version, then payload type, then route type — the reverse of the order you usually think about them in. That ordering isn't arbitrary: route type sits in the low bits because it's what a repeater masks off first, and it's the only field a repeater strictly needs to do its job.

The two TRANSPORT_* variants behave identically to their plain counterparts but insert four extra bytes after the header — a 16-bit code derived from region scope, plus a reserved one. They exist so a broadcast can be confined to a geographic region instead of leaking across an entire interconnected mesh.

Payload version is effectively always 0 today. Version 1 is reserved for a future format with 2-byte source and destination hashes and a 4-byte MAC. It's a growth hinge for when one byte of addressing stops being enough — the same pressure that produced multibyte path hashes, one layer down.

Reading it by eye

After a while you recognise header bytes on sight. 0x09 is a flooded text message. 0x11 is a flooded advert. 0x0E is a direct ACK. Those three cover most of what a busy channel carries.

Offset 0x01, or 0x05 with transport codesThe path_length byte

not a byte count
Byte0x02 Hops2 ×1 byte =2 path bytes

This is the field people misread. It is not the length of the path in bytes. The low six bits hold a hop count of 0–63; the top two hold the hash size minus one. The receiver multiplies them to find how many bytes of path to actually consume before the payload starts. Get this wrong and every byte after it is garbage — which is exactly what happens to firmware older than v1.14.0 when it meets a multibyte packet.

Each hop's entry is the first N bytes of that repeater's Ed25519 public key: a truncated identity fingerprint, not a hash of the route. Since the path field is capped at 64 bytes, hash size and reach trade directly against each other.

Zero is a real value

A path length of 0x00 on a DIRECT packet means zero-hop: transmitted, but no repeater will forward it. It's how you talk to a neighbour you can hear yourself, and how repeaters advertise locally without waking the whole region.

With 254 usable one-byte IDs, prefix collisions are inevitable past a few dozen repeaters, and they fail in a way that resists diagnosis: two repeaters share a prefix, both match the front of a direct-routed path, both strip themselves, and the packet leaves in a direction nobody planned. The Czech network passed 255 repeaters in early 2026 — more nodes than the address space can represent at all. Multibyte hashes landed in firmware v1.14.0; repeaters on that or later forward all three sizes regardless of their own setting, but older firmware drops 2- and 3-byte packets silently, with no error and no NACK.

Layer 02 · meshFlood once, then go direct

path discovery

MeshCore is hybrid. The first message to a contact floods the mesh and collects breadcrumbs; everything after follows the learned route hop by hop. Step through it and watch the path field grow on the way out, then get eaten on the way back.

Routeflood Path field
Notes

Flood

Path starts empty. Every repeater that hears the packet checks its seen-table, appends its own hash, and rebroadcasts exactly once. Duplicates arriving later are dropped, so the path that reaches the destination is the trail of whichever copy won the race — not necessarily the shortest one on a map.

Path return

The destination replies with a PAYLOAD_TYPE_PATH packet sent DIRECT along the reversed route, and can piggyback an ACK or response inside its extra field. Confirming the message and teaching the sender its route costs one packet, not two.

Direct

Each repeater compares only the first hash in the path against its own. On a match it shifts the path left by one hash and retransmits, so the path shrinks as the packet travels and an empty path means "next stop is the destination". Direct traffic is queued at priority 0 with no added delay.

Backoff

Flood retransmissions are deliberately staggered. The firmware estimates the airtime of the packet it's about to repeat, pads it by about 4%, and multiplies by a small random integer — so neighbours that heard the same broadcast don't answer in unison.

There's a fourth mode worth knowing: PAYLOAD_TYPE_TRACE. A trace packet is sent DIRECT along a chosen path, and each hop that matches appends its measured SNR — stored as the signal-to-noise ratio times four, in a signed byte — before forwarding. When it reaches the end you get a per-hop quality profile of a route, which is how you find the weak link in a chain that mostly works.

Layer 03 · identityWho you are is a key

Ed25519 · X25519 · AES-128

A MeshCore node has no address assigned to it. On first boot it generates an Ed25519 keypair, and that key is its identity — for signing, for deriving encryption secrets, and for routing. The one-byte path hash and the one-byte source and destination hashes in a payload are all just the front of that same public key, truncated hard enough to fit.

Identity

Ed25519 keypair generated from hardware entropy at first boot. Adverts carry the full 32-byte public key and a signature over the whole advert, so a node's name and position can't be spoofed by someone who doesn't hold the private key.

Shared secret

Keys are converted to X25519 form and combined by ECDH: your private key plus their public key yields the same 32 bytes as theirs plus yours. Nothing is exchanged on air to set this up — the advert already carried everything needed, and the result is cached per contact.

Message protection

Payloads are encrypted with AES-128, zero-padded to the block size, and carry a truncated HMAC-SHA256 as a 2-byte MAC. The receiver checks the MAC first and only then decrypts. Two bytes is small — it catches corruption and casual tampering, not a determined attacker.

The same packet, two ways of looking at it

Always in the clear

Routing has to work on nodes that can't read the message, so all of this stays plaintext. Anyone listening learns that someone whose key starts with 0x7C sent something to someone whose key starts with 0xB4, via these repeaters, at this moment, with this much text in it.

Encrypted to the recipient

Channels are a secret, not a network

A group message is addressed by a single byte: the first byte of SHA-256 of the channel key. Anyone holding that key decrypts it; anyone who doesn't sees noise with a matching prefix. There's no membership, no join, and no way to remove someone except by changing the key and redistributing it.

Talking to strangers

ANON_REQ exists because you sometimes need to contact a node that has never heard of you — logging into a room server, say. It carries a full 32-byte public key inline instead of a one-byte hash, so the receiver can derive the shared secret on the spot without a prior advert.

Worth being clear-eyed about the limits. AES-128 in ECB mode means identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks, which leaks structure in repetitive messages. There's no per-message nonce, and the MAC is two bytes. The design is a reasonable fit for 8-bit-class embedded hardware and casual privacy; it is not the same thing as a modern authenticated encryption scheme, and it shouldn't be described as one.

Header bits 2–5Sixteen kinds of payload

pick one

Every multi-byte integer is little-endian. Source and destination hashes inside a payload are always one byte in payload version 1, whatever path hash mode the network runs — the path hash size affects routing only, and the two are easy to confuse.

Note how many types share the same four-byte envelope: destination hash, source hash, two-byte MAC. Requests, responses, text messages and returned paths are structurally identical on the wire; only the decrypted contents differ.

Input · hex0 bytes

The parse, in four moves

01  Read byte 0. Mask & 0x03 for route type, >>2 & 0x0F for payload type, >>6 for version.

02  If route type is 0 or 3, skip four transport-code bytes. Otherwise you're already at the path length byte.

03  Read the path length byte. Hash size is (b>>6)+1, hop count is b & 0x3F. Multiply them and skip that many bytes of path.

04  Everything left is payload, and its shape is whatever the payload type in step 1 said it would be.

What you can actually read off the air

More than you'd expect. Route type, payload type, hop count and the full path are plaintext by necessity. Adverts are almost entirely readable: node name in ASCII, coordinates as signed 32-bit integers scaled by a million, and a role flag saying whether it's a client, repeater, room server or sensor.

What you don't get is content. Text messages, requests and responses are AES blocks whose key you'd need a private key to derive. You can map a network completely — who repeats for whom, where the infrastructure sits, when it's busy — without decrypting a single message.

Details worth knowing

field notes
  • An ACK has no addressFour bytes of CRC over the original message timestamp, text and sender public key. The sender recognises its own message by matching the checksum, so no addressing fields are needed at all — and no one else can tell what was acknowledged.
  • Adverts are the fat ones32-byte public key, 4-byte timestamp, 64-byte signature: 100 bytes before a name or coordinates. This is the heaviest routine traffic on a channel, which is why advert intervals matter more to congestion than message rate does. Repeater defaults sit in the tens of hours for flood adverts.
  • Long paths cost you wordsEvery hop eats budget from the same 255 bytes. At 12 hops in 3-byte mode you've spent 36 bytes on routing before writing a character. PAYLOAD_TYPE_MULTIPART exists for when that isn't enough.
  • Clients don't repeatCompanion firmware sends and receives but never forwards. Repeating is a deliberate role held by infrastructure, which is the main structural reason flood traffic stays bounded compared to a mesh where every handset rebroadcasts.
  • Text messages carry a retry counterThe byte after the timestamp packs txt_type in its upper six bits and an attempt number 0–3 in the lower two, so a retransmission is distinguishable from a duplicate. A txt_type of 1 means the text is a CLI command, not a message.
  • Repeaters keep clocksEncrypted payloads carry timestamps to resist replay, and adverts are signed over their timestamp. A repeater with a badly wrong clock causes symptoms that look like radio problems, which is why RTC modules show up on serious builds.
  • Region scope is a packet field, not a frequencyTransport codes let a mesh that spans interconnected regions confine a broadcast, rather than relying on radio range to do it.
